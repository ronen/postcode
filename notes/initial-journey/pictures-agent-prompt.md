# Pictures experiment: views of a retained working account

You are helping test whether an explicit account of software understanding can
support useful pictorial explanations for a human. This is a design/research
exercise, not a request to implement a GUI or modify the software.

You are receiving two accompanying files:

- `cockatiel-working-account.md`
- `visual-language-references.md`

If either file is missing, ask for it before starting. Otherwise proceed with a
first version using your judgment; do not require a preliminary approval round.

## Starting material and boundaries

Treat the working account as your sole source of Cockatiel-specific information.
It is a qualified account produced by an earlier investigation, not verified
ground truth. Do not supplement it with your own remembered Cockatiel facts.

You may consult the external works listed in the visual-language references
to inform how you represent information. Do not retrieve the original dialogue,
other chats, PostCode project files, Cockatiel source or documentation, or the
linked issue. Links in the working account are provenance pointers, not permission
to fetch additional context for this exercise. Do not ask another agent to recover
that context for you.

If a reference is inaccessible, record that briefly and continue with what is
available. If the account lacks information needed for a picture, identify the
gap. You may use explicitly labeled illustrative examples, provided their
assumptions are stated and they do not silently add software behavior.

## Assignment

Create a small, coherent set of actual pictures explaining the understanding
expressed at the end of the working account. Aim for roughly three to five views
or storyboard panels, choosing the subjects and arrangement that best communicate
the account's important ideas.

You may tell a new explanatory story. Do not claim to reconstruct the original
conversation: you have not been given it.

The audience wants to understand and guide software development without needing
to read its implementation language. The pictures should help the person build
an overall understanding, inspect relevant behavior and relationships, and notice
useful questions. They should be understandable without following evidence links.

Make them meaningfully pictorial: use recognizable objects, functional symbols,
spatial arrangements, successive states or other visual devices where helpful.
Avoid merely placing paragraphs in boxes or adding decorative icons to a graph.
Brief labels, captions and legends are welcome. Do not force everything into a
metaphor when a conventional diagram communicates it better.

Use the references selectively. You do not need one set of pictures per visual
scheme, or a mashup of every approach. Keep recurring subjects recognizable
across views. A focused alternative treatment is welcome if it exposes a real
tradeoff, but a broad notation comparison is outside this first pass.

## Preserve the account's distinctions

Keep existing behavior, proposed changes, human preferences and unresolved
questions distinguishable. Preserve qualifications that materially affect what
a reader would conclude. Do not make illustrative behavior look like a recorded
execution or a successful verification.

The pictures are views of the supplied account. If making them requires a new
inference, label it and explain its basis. If a metaphor suggests something
unsupported, revise it or make its limitation apparent. Readability matters more
than exhaustive coverage.

## Deliverables

1. The rendered pictures, with short captions explaining what each helps the
   person understand or decide. Supply portable files and editable sources where
   practical; a textual description of intended pictures alone is not sufficient.
   You may use rendering code or image tools without building an application.
2. A brief companion note mapping each view's substantive claims to headings or
   passages in the supplied account. Keep this traceability outside the main
   pictures unless a qualification needs to be visible there.
3. A short assessment of the account as starting material: what it supported,
   what was missing or ambiguous, any assumptions or new inferences, and risks of
   misleading visual interpretation. Name the visual references actually used.
4. A few concrete questions we could ask a viewer to test understanding and
   discover misconceptions, without claiming that the pictures have already
   demonstrated improved comprehension.

Record the supplied account version and keep the input files unchanged so we can
bring your outputs back to the original exploration and assess what transferred.
